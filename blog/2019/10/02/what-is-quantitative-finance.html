What is quantitative finance? A timeline of the ideas behind the models

October 02, 2019 · … min read
quantitative finance history portfolio theory asset pricing derivatives

What does a quant actually do? One person might estimate the risk of a portfolio, another might price an option, and a third might test whether a trading signal survives transaction costs. They share a habit: describe a financial question in terms of data, uncertainty, and a model whose assumptions can be challenged. Quantitative finance is the use of mathematics, statistics, economics, and computation to make those descriptions useful for investment, pricing, and risk decisions. It is not synonymous with automated trading, nor is it a promise that markets obey elegant equations.

There was no single birthday for the field. Probability and insurance mathematics came before the models below, and traders priced options long before a modern formula existed. This selective timeline follows influential published contributions to modern financial modeling, not claims that their authors invented every underlying idea. Each milestone includes a bibliographic reference with pages or a named section to help you check the account; journal articles do not have book chapters.

The ideas in chronological order

1900: Bachelier puts speculation into a probability model

In Théorie de la spéculation, Louis Bachelier treated changes in prices as random and applied probability to the valuation of contingent claims (Bachelier, 1900). His model used arithmetic fluctuations, which can produce negative modeled prices, so it is a starting point, not the stock-price model we would blindly use today. The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences recounts both the contribution and that limitation in its history of option valuation (The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 1997).

Bibliographic reference: Bachelier’s journal article, pp. 21–86, and the Academy’s “The history of option valuation” section. Read the 1900 article or the Academy’s historical account.

1952: Markowitz makes diversification a calculation

Harry Markowitz’s Portfolio Selection argued that the risk of a portfolio depends on how asset returns move together, not just on the risk of each security separately (Markowitz, 1952). In a mean-variance description, weights $w$ and the return covariance matrix $\Sigma$ determine portfolio variance $w^{\mathsf T}\Sigma w$. This gave investment managers an explicit way to compare expected return with risk instead of treating diversification as a slogan. It remains a model: estimates of expected returns and covariances can be unstable.

Bibliographic reference: Markowitz’s journal article, pp. 77–91, and “Harry M. Markowitz” in the Academy’s 1990 account (The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 1990). The Academy also points to the longer treatment in his Portfolio Selection: Efficient Diversification of Investments (1959); I am attributing the dated milestone to the 1952 article, not the later book.

1958: Modigliani and Miller ask what a financing choice really changes

Franco Modigliani and Merton Miller used a deliberately idealized capital market to analyze whether changing a firm’s mix of debt and equity changes its value (Modigliani & Miller, 1958). Their point was not that taxes, distress costs, or financing constraints are imaginary; it was to establish a benchmark so that departures from it could be identified and priced. Quantitative finance is often as much about finding the value of a violated assumption as about solving an equation.

Bibliographic reference: The Cost of Capital, Corporation Finance and the Theory of Investment, pp. 261–297, and “Merton Miller” in the Academy’s 1990 account (The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 1990).

1964: Sharpe connects portfolio risk to expected return

William Sharpe’s capital asset pricing model (CAPM) connects the risk premium on an asset to its exposure to a market portfolio rather than to its total standalone volatility (Sharpe, 1964). In the familiar representation, $E[R_i]-R_f=\beta_i(E[R_m]-R_f)$. Beta is a covariance-based measure of market risk: idiosyncratic risk can be diversified, while systematic exposure cannot be removed merely by holding more stocks. This is a theoretical equilibrium statement under strong assumptions, not a guaranteed empirical pricing rule.

Bibliographic reference: Capital Asset Prices: A Theory of Market Equilibrium under Conditions of Risk, pp. 425–442, and “William F. Sharpe” in the Academy’s 1990 account (The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 1990).

1970: Fama organizes the evidence on efficient markets

Eugene Fama’s review Efficient Capital Markets brought together a framework for examining how information is reflected in prices, including weak, semi-strong, and strong-form tests (Fama, 1970). This is a program for asking which information and which test, not an assertion that every price is always correct. The question of whether a profitable signal survives the costs and constraints of implementation is central to quantitative investing.

Bibliographic reference: Fama’s journal review, pp. 383–417. The Academy’s 2013 account discusses the empirical asset-pricing contribution (The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 2013).

1973: Black, Scholes, and Merton price by hedging

Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published an option-pricing formula, while Robert Merton developed and extended the hedging argument (Black & Scholes, 1973) (Merton, 1973). The change in perspective was profound: under their model’s assumptions, a continuously adjusted hedge and the absence of arbitrage link a derivative’s price to its underlying asset without having to guess an investor-specific risk premium. Continuous trading, constant volatility, and frictionless markets are approximations, so the formula is a benchmark rather than a description of every option market.

Bibliographic reference: Black and Scholes, The Pricing of Options and Corporate Liabilities, pp. 637–654; Merton, Theory of Rational Option Pricing, pp. 141–183. For a concise annotated derivation, read “The Black-Scholes formula” in the Academy’s 1997 background material (The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences, 1997).

1979: Cox, Ross, and Rubinstein make option pricing a tree

The binomial approach made the no-arbitrage hedge visible one short step at a time (Cox et al., 1979). At each node, the stock can move up or down; a replicating position in stock and a risk-free asset determines the option value. Repeating those steps gives a practical numerical method for many contracts, including early-exercise decisions, and makes the link between discrete models and continuous-time pricing easier to see.

Bibliographic reference: Option Pricing: A Simplified Approach, pp. 229–263. Read the publisher’s article record.

1979: Harrison and Kreps put no-arbitrage on firm mathematical ground

J. Michael Harrison and David Kreps studied multiperiod securities markets, asking when prices rule out a self-financing strategy that produces a sure gain from nothing (Harrison & Kreps, 1979). In their model, absence of arbitrage is characterized through an equivalent probability measure under which appropriately discounted security prices are martingales. This is the rigorous bridge from the replicating-portfolio intuition above to risk-neutral pricing: probabilities used to price hedged claims need not be forecasts of actual market outcomes. The result depends on the market and admissible-trading assumptions of the paper; it is not a claim that any quoted price can be justified by changing probabilities.

Bibliographic reference: Martingales and Arbitrage in Multiperiod Securities Markets, pp. 381–408. Read the original paper’s record.

1981–83: Harrison and Pliska extend the pricing logic to continuous trading

Harrison and Stanley Pliska developed the stochastic-integral framework needed to express dynamically traded portfolios in continuous time (Harrison & Pliska, 1981). Their subsequent complete-markets model makes the second question precise: can the claim actually be replicated? (Harrison & Pliska, 1983) Under the relevant assumptions, a replicable terminal payoff $X$ has a price given by its discounted expectation under a martingale pricing measure; in a complete market, that pricing measure is unique. Without completeness, no-arbitrage alone generally does not select one price for every unhedgeable claim. This is why the mathematics matters to a derivatives desk: it tells us when a model yields a unique hedge and price, and when additional modeling choices or risk premia are unavoidable.

Bibliographic reference: Martingales and Stochastic Integrals in the Theory of Continuous Trading, pp. 215–260, and A Stochastic Calculus Model of Continuous Trading: Complete Markets, pp. 313–316. Read the 1981 paper’s record and the 1983 paper’s record.

2010s: Data and computation widen the toolkit

Faster market data and computing made it practical to update models and send orders algorithmically. That is broader than high-frequency trading: a systematic portfolio may rebalance once a month, while a low-latency market maker operates on a much shorter clock. Hendershott, Jones, and Menkveld studied algorithmic trading and liquidity rather than claiming every algorithm discovers an arbitrage (Hendershott et al., 2011). By 2018, machine-learning methods were also being applied to financial data, with particular attention to backtesting and the danger of learning patterns that will not survive out of sample (López de Prado, 2018). Computation changed the scale of experimentation, not the obligation to test assumptions.

Bibliographic reference: Does Algorithmic Trading Improve Liquidity?, pp. 1–33, and López de Prado, Advances in Financial Machine Learning, Chapter 1, “Financial Machine Learning as a Distinct Subject”. Read the paper’s record and the book’s publisher listing.

What quants are trying to accomplish

It helps to distinguish two probability perspectives, often called $P$ and $Q$. Under a real-world measure $P$, we estimate what may happen: returns, losses, default frequencies, and whether a strategy has predictive power. Under a risk-neutral pricing measure $Q$, we value replicable claims consistently with traded prices and no-arbitrage assumptions. $Q$ is not a better forecast of what investors believe will happen; it is a pricing device. Harrison and Kreps, followed by Harrison and Pliska, made the assumptions behind this distinction much more precise (Harrison & Kreps, 1979; Harrison & Pliska, 1981).

  • Derivatives pricing: value and hedge contingent payoffs using no-arbitrage models, lattices, stochastic calculus, or simulation. This is usually a $Q$-measure question; a model value is conditional on its inputs and hedgeability.
  • Risk management: measure exposures and possible losses with scenarios, Value at Risk, expected shortfall, and stress tests. Historical loss probabilities are $P$-measure estimates, while market-implied values can inform the risks of a derivatives book. Neither measure replaces stress testing.
  • Portfolio construction: allocate capital across assets given objectives, forecasts, correlations, liquidity, and constraints. Mean-variance optimization is one starting point, not a substitute for checking estimation error (Markowitz, 1952).
  • Systematic trading: test signals, execution costs, and position rules against out-of-sample data before committing capital. Models of actual returns and trading outcomes generally concern $P$; automated execution alone does not imply an edge.

Where to read next

For readers who want to take the mathematics further, these are useful paths, not a ranking of all finance books:

  • Options and hedging: John Hull’s Options, Futures, and Other Derivatives (Hull, 2018) develops the contracts, binomial trees, and Black–Scholes–Merton model. Start with its chapters “Binomial Trees” and “The Black–Scholes–Merton Model”.
  • Pricing foundations: Steven Shreve’s Stochastic Calculus for Finance I and II (Shreve, 2004; Shreve, 2004) move from a discrete binomial setting to continuous-time stochastic calculus and risk-neutral valuation. Start with Volume I, Chapter 1, “The Binomial No-Arbitrage Pricing Model,” before Volume II’s continuous-time chapters.
  • Portfolio research: Richard Grinold and Ronald Kahn’s Active Portfolio Management (Grinold & Kahn, 1999) connects forecasts, risk, and portfolio construction. Begin with its opening “Introduction” chapter and then the chapters on forecasts and risk.

What the timeline leaves us with

The same mathematical tools can serve investment, pricing, and risk, but the question comes first. A model can be internally consistent and still fail when its market assumptions, data, or implementation change. For me, a useful quantitative model is one whose inputs, assumptions, and failure modes can be explained to the person who has to make a decision with it.
